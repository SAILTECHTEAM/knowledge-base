# YOLO Appendix: Superseded Tricks and Engineering Trade-offs

:::note[Context]
This is a companion appendix to [Part 2](./v4-v5-integration). It covers techniques tested in YOLOv4 that were later superseded. No companion PDF notes apply here.
:::

In [Part 2](./v4-v5-integration), the core methods that became standard in modern single-stage object detection were examined — CSPNet, PANet, SPPF, Mosaic augmentation, CIoU loss, and label smoothing. During the development of YOLOv4, however, the authors evaluated many additional candidate techniques from the research literature.

While these techniques produced measurable accuracy improvements in initial ablation studies, subsequent detector iterations replaced most of them with hardware-friendly, computationally cheaper, or more direct alternatives.

Reviewing these superseded approaches clarifies the stringent **engineering trade-offs** of production-grade object detection: theoretical gains must survive the triple constraint of **computational efficiency (FLOPs and memory footprint), hardware memory access patterns (cache locality and quantization support), and crowding-out effects from strong data augmentations**.

This appendix examines the underlying mechanics of Self-Adversarial Training (SAT), DropBlock, the Mish activation function, the Spatial Attention Module (SAM), and DIoU-NMS, explaining why each technique fell out of favor in mainstream detector design.

---

## Self-Adversarial Training (SAT)

### Mechanism and Mathematical Formulation

Self-Adversarial Training (SAT) is a training-time data augmentation technique where the network generates adversarial hard examples against itself. Unlike conventional adversarial defense, SAT does not protect against malicious attacks; instead, it uses adversarial perturbations to expose representation blind spots under current weights.

SAT alternates between two distinct phases:

1. **Phase 1: freeze network weights and generate adversarial perturbations in input space.**
   Holding model parameters $\theta$ fixed, compute the gradient of the detection loss $L$ with respect to the input image $X$, denoted $\nabla_X L(\theta, X)$. Using a single-step Fast Gradient Sign Method (FGSM), inject a small perturbation into the original image in the direction of gradient ascent:

   $$\tilde{X} = X + \epsilon \cdot \text{sign}\left(\nabla_X L(\theta, X)\right)$$

   The perturbation magnitude $\epsilon$ is strictly constrained so that the noise is imperceptible to human vision, yet strong enough in feature space to cause missed detections or bounding box drift under the current weights.
2. **Phase 2: fix the adversarial sample and update network weights normally.**
   Pass the synthesized adversarial image $\tilde{X}$ through the network, execute standard forward and backward passes, and update model parameters along gradient descent:

   $$\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta \cdot \nabla_\theta L(\theta, \tilde{X})$$

This optimization forms a standard min-max game: $\min_\theta \max_{\|\delta\| \le \epsilon} L(\theta, X + \delta)$. Single-step perturbations smooth local non-convex loss surfaces, improving model robustness to small geometric and textural variations.

### Superseding Factors and Architectural Insights

- **Doubled training compute**: Phase 1 requires a full forward pass and an input-level backward pass across high-resolution images. Every training iteration consumes more than double the computational time and memory bandwidth of standard training.
- **Crowding out by input-level augmentations**: SAT uses costly gradient backpropagation to synthesize hard examples at the feature level. Later input-level geometric and semantic mixing methods — such as Mosaic, MixUp, and Copy-Paste — generate complex contexts and occluded hard examples on the CPU with simple tensor concatenation and zero backpropagation. Given this efficiency gap, costly adversarial backpropagation was abandoned for cheaper input-level augmentations.

## DropBlock: Continuous Spatial Block Regularization

### Mechanism and Mathematical Formulation

In fully connected networks, Dropout breaks feature co-adaptation by randomly zeroing out individual neuron activations independently. When applied directly to fully convolutional feature maps, standard Dropout largely fails.

Convolutional feature maps exhibit strong **spatial continuity**. Adjacent activation units share receptive fields and semantic context. If an isolated activation point is zeroed out, neighboring activations pass identical edge and texture cues to downstream layers. The network circumvents the dropout mask through local interpolation, defeating the goal of learning distributed representations.

![DropBlock mechanism: unlike Dropout which drops isolated feature points, DropBlock drops contiguous spatial feature blocks](/img/cv_img/yolo-series/dropblock-explain.png)

> Source: Ghiasi et al., *DropBlock: A regularization method for convolutional networks*, Fig. 1, https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.12890

DropBlock generalizes the dropped unit from discrete points to **contiguous spatial blocks**. On a feature map of size $H \times W$, DropBlock randomly samples block centers with probability $\gamma$ and sets an $S_{\text{block}} \times S_{\text{block}}$ spatial mask around each sampled center to zero:

$$\gamma = \frac{1 - \text{keep\_prob}}{S_{\text{block}}^2} \cdot \frac{H \cdot W}{(H - S_{\text{block}} + 1)(W - S_{\text{block}} + 1)}$$

To ensure consistent output expectation between training and inference, retained activations are normalized by dividing by $(1 - \gamma)$. Erasing contiguous spatial regions prevents convolutional kernels from over-relying on a single dominant region (such as a bird's head), forcing the network to attend to complementary discriminative cues across the entire object (such as wings, claws, or feather textures).

### Superseding Factors and Architectural Insights

- **Superseded by strong input augmentations**: DropBlock removes local feature information within intermediate layers to encourage distributed representations. Input-level augmentations such as Random Erasing, CutOut, and Mosaic directly simulate large-scale object occlusion on raw pixels with clearer physical meaning.
- **Underfitting risks from compounded regularization**: when input pipelines already apply aggressive Mosaic stitching and photometric distortions, adding DropBlock within feature maps frequently destroys weak activation signals for small targets in deep layers. This compounding regularization impedes convergence over standard training schedules, risking severe underfitting.

## Mish Activation Function

### Mechanism and Mathematical Formulation

During YOLOv4 development, single-stage detectors predominantly used ReLU or LeakyReLU activations. ReLU truncates the negative half-space strictly to zero ($\text{ReLU}(x) = \max(0, x)$). Its first derivative contains a step discontinuity at $x=0$, which can lead to dead neurons when activations collapse to zero.

YOLOv4 evaluated and adopted the **Mish activation function** across its CSPDarknet53 backbone:

$$\text{Mish}(x) = x \cdot \tanh(\text{softplus}(x)) = x \cdot \tanh\left(\ln(1 + e^x)\right)$$

Mish exhibits three mathematical properties:

1. **Continuous differentiability and smooth curvature**: Mish is infinitely differentiable ($C^\infty$ smooth) across the entire real line. Eliminating first-derivative discontinuities prevents sharp oscillations on optimization surfaces, stabilizing gradient propagation across deep networks.
2. **Non-monotonicity with negative gradient flow**: Mish is non-monotonic on the negative half-space, reaching a local minimum of approximately $-0.3088$ near $x \approx -1.192$. This non-monotonic dip allows small negative gradients to pass through the network, providing mild implicit regularization.
3. **Unbounded above and bounded below**: an unbounded positive domain prevents gradient saturation in deep layers, while a bounded negative domain provides structural regularization.

### Superseding Factors and Architectural Insights

- **Hardware operator complexity and inference latency**: despite theoretical benefits and small mAP gains on compact models, Mish nests three transcendental non-linear functions — exponential ($\exp$), natural logarithm ($\ln$), and hyperbolic tangent ($\tanh$). On GPUs, NPUs, and DSPs, evaluating transcendental functions breaks instruction pipelines in CUDA cores and specialized vector units, inflating inference latency and memory bandwidth consumption.
- **Superior engineering profile of SiLU (Swish)**: YOLOv5 and all subsequent YOLO iterations transitioned entirely to **SiLU ($\text{SiLU}(x) = x \cdot \sigma(x) = \frac{x}{1 + e^{-x}}$)**. SiLU shares nearly identical mathematical geometry with Mish (smoothness, non-monotonicity, and negative gradient flow), but relies on a single sigmoid computation. SiLU executes with lower latency, lower memory traffic, and out-of-the-box support across hardware quantization runtimes (including TensorRT INT8 post-training quantization). Mish validated the utility of smooth self-gated non-linearities, but SiLU delivered the better hardware implementation.

## Spatial Attention Module (SAM)

### Mechanism and Mathematical Formulation

In YOLOv4, concatenating the four pooling branches ($1\times1, 5\times5, 9\times9, 13\times13$) inside the SPP block expands channel dimensionality to 2048. To filter redundant representations before passing features into PANet, YOLOv4 evaluated a modified **Spatial Attention Module (SAM)**.

![YOLOv4 modified SAM: point-wise spatial attention weighting](https://ar5iv.org/html/2004.10934/assets/fig/sam.png)

> Source: Bochkovskiy et al., YOLOv4, Fig. 5 Modified SAM, https://ar5iv.org/html/2004.10934

Standard CBAM spatial attention computes global average and max pooling across channels to produce two $H \times W \times 1$ feature maps, concatenates them, and applies a large convolutional kernel to produce a 2D spatial mask. To reduce computational overhead, YOLOv4 reformulated SAM into a point-wise spatial transformation:

$$M_{\text{spatial}} = \sigma\left(\text{Conv}_{1 \times 1}(X)\right),\quad Y = X \odot M_{\text{spatial}}$$

A $1 \times 1$ convolution maps the input tensor directly to a single-channel spatial map, passed through sigmoid activation to yield spatial weights $M_{\text{spatial}} \in (0, 1)^{H \times W \times 1}$. The input tensor is then modulated via element-wise broadcast multiplication.

### Superseding Factors and Architectural Insights

- **Representational dimension mismatch**: feature redundancy following SPP pooling stems primarily from **channel collinearity** (induced by concatenating four parallel pooling paths), not coarse 2D spatial layout. Point-wise spatial attention applies a single spatial weight uniformly across all channels, leaving channel-level redundancies unaddressed.
- **Evolution of attention architectures**: subsequent architectures showed that a simple $1 \times 1$ transition convolution provides efficient channel compression after SPP. Dedicated attention mechanisms evolved toward more refined designs — such as YOLOv11's C2PSA module and YOLOv12's area attention — rendering simple point-wise spatial gating obsolete.

## DIoU-NMS (Distance-IoU Non-Maximum Suppression)

### Mechanism and Mathematical Formulation

Standard greedy NMS uses bounding box IoU as the sole criterion for suppressing redundant predictions. For candidate box $M$ with the highest confidence score, any overlapping box $b_i$ satisfying $\text{IoU}(M, b_i) \ge \text{Threshold}$ is suppressed (score set to zero).

In crowded scenes or dense object distributions (such as parallel vehicles or adjacent pedestrians), ground-truth boxes of two physically distinct objects often have an IoU exceeding the suppression threshold. Standard NMS discards the lower-confidence prediction, causing missed detections in crowded areas.

DIoU-NMS incorporates normalized Euclidean distance between bounding box center points into the suppression criterion:

$$s_i = \begin{cases} s_i, & \text{IoU}(M, b_i) - \mathcal{R}_{\text{DIoU}}(M, b_i) < \text{Threshold} \\ 0, & \text{IoU}(M, b_i) - \mathcal{R}_{\text{DIoU}}(M, b_i) \ge \text{Threshold} \end{cases}$$

The penalty term is defined as $\mathcal{R}_{\text{DIoU}}(M, b_i) = \frac{\rho^2(b_M, b_i)}{c^2}$, where $\rho(\cdot)$ denotes Euclidean distance between box center points, and $c$ is the diagonal length of the smallest enclosing box. When two candidate boxes have distant center points despite high area overlap, the penalty term lowers the effective suppression metric, preserving separate overlapping instances during post-processing.

### Superseding Factors and Architectural Insights

- **Inherent limits of heuristic post-processing**: DIoU-NMS remains a heuristic greedy set-covering algorithm. While center distance helps in mild overlap scenarios, it deteriorates under severe occlusion or extreme scale disparities, introducing new false positives or missed detections.
- **Transition to end-to-end NMS-free architectures**: engineering practice established that **patching redundant predictions in post-processing addresses symptoms rather than root causes**. Redundant bounding boxes originate from one-to-many label assignment during training. The definitive solution is transitioning to end-to-end one-to-one matching and dual label assignment architectures (such as YOLOv10's consistent dual assignments). By training the network to produce a single high-quality prediction per object instance, modern detectors eliminate NMS entirely.

## Summary: Evolutionary Trajectory of Superseded Techniques

| Technique | Problem Addressed | Theoretical / Empirical Gain | Primary Reason for Supersession / Replacement | Architectural Insight |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Self-Adversarial Training (SAT)** | Hard example mining; smoothing non-convex loss surfaces | Improved generalization and perturbation robustness | Doubled training compute; replaced by efficient **Mosaic / MixUp** | Complex gradient-based hard example mining is less efficient than input-level geometric mixing. |
| **DropBlock** | Breaking spatial correlation in convolutional features | Forced network to learn distributed discriminative cues | Input-level **Mosaic / Random Erasing** provide sufficient occlusion regularization | Input augmentations crowd out internal feature regularization; avoid stacking redundant regularizers. |
| **Mish Activation** | Smooth continuous differentiability; avoiding dead neurons | Smooth optimization gradients; minor mAP gains | High GPU latency and quantization complexity from nested $\exp/\ln/\tanh$; replaced by **SiLU** | Theoretical smoothness must align with hardware instruction sets and INT8 quantization workflows. |
| **Spatial Attention (SAM)** | Feature redundancy suppression after SPP concatenation | Injected 2D spatial saliency weights into feature maps | SPP redundancy is channel-centric; replaced by **$1\times1$ projection and modern self-attention** | Point-wise spatial gating cannot resolve channel collinearity; attention shifted toward structured self-attention. |
| **DIoU-NMS** | False-positive suppression in dense, overlapping objects | Improved recall under proximate occlusion | Still a greedy heuristic; replaced by **end-to-end NMS-free designs (YOLOv10)** | Eliminating duplicate predictions via one-to-one label assignment beats post-processing heuristics. |

Analyzing these superseded techniques illustrates the foundational design principle of modern single-stage detectors: **architectural progress is not a pursuit of mathematical complexity, but a continuous engineering optimization balancing representational capacity, training compute, hardware memory latency, and deployment constraints**.
