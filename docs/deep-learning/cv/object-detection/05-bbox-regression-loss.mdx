# Deconstructing Object Detection (4): Box Representation and Regression Loss

Evaluation cares about overlap; training once cared about edge differences. A 2-pixel drift on one edge of that small sign ahead is nearly invisible across the whole image yet eats an eighth of its width; the same 2 pixels on those two pedestrians ahead barely register. And when predicted boxes miss entirely, coordinate gaps remain while IoU is already zero.

Evaluation inspects predicted-versus-true region overlap, but training that stares only at four edge-value gaps optimizes against its own yardstick. Localization in detection kept patching this training-evaluation misalignment, ultimately pushing boundary representation from single-point estimation to probability distributions.

## Geometric parameterization: absolute pixels to relative space

In computer vision, a horizontal rectangle's rawest image-space form is absolute pixel coordinates — usually corner vertices $(x_1, y_1, x_2, y_2)$ or center-plus-size $(c_x, c_y, w, h)$. On $640\times 640$ inputs these values fall in $[0, 640]$.

Asking a network's linear output layer to regress absolute pixels directly is numerically fragile. A frame holding hundred-pixel pedestrians alongside a dozen-pixel sign produces errors of tens to hundreds for large scales versus fractions for small ones. Backpropagation drowns small-object gradients under large-object dominance. To make predictions comparable across scales, boxes need dimensionless, relatively reconstructed parameterization.

### Relative transforms under anchors

In anchor-based systems, each prediction position presets benchmark boxes of fixed sizes. With prior center/size $(x_a, y_a, w_a, h_a)$ and truth $(x_{gt}, y_{gt}, w_{gt}, h_{gt})$, the network predicts a four-dimensional offset vector $\boldsymbol{t} = (t_x, t_y, t_w, t_h)$ instead of absolute borders.

Mathematically:

$$
\begin{aligned}
t_x &= \frac{x_{gt} - x_a}{w_a}, \quad &t_y = \frac{y_{gt} - y_a}{h_a} \\
t_w &= \ln\left(\frac{w_{gt}}{w_a}\right), \quad &t_h = \ln\left(\frac{h_{gt}}{h_a}\right)
\end{aligned}
$$

The inverse transform recovering predicted coordinates $(\hat{x}, \hat{y}, \hat{w}, \hat{h})$ from network output $\boldsymbol{t}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat{x} &= x_a + w_a \cdot t_x, \quad &\hat{y} = y_a + h_a \cdot t_y \\
\hat{w} &= w_a \cdot \exp(t_w), \quad &\hat{h} = h_a \cdot \exp(t_h)
\end{aligned}
$$

Two design considerations sit inside these formulas:

1. **Scale-normalized center shifts**: $t_x$ and $t_y$ measure relative center offsets divided by the prior's own $w_a$ and $h_a$. Whatever the object size, equal relative shifts land $t_x, t_y$ in the same magnitude band (usually near $[-0.5, 0.5]$), killing large objects' positional gradient monopoly.
2. **Logarithmic nonlinear width/height mapping**: log-transforming size ratios $\ln(w / w_a)$ first guarantees inverse-transformed sizes via $\exp(t_w)$ stay positive — no negative dimensions. Second, it converts nonlinear multiplicative relationships into linear additive gaps. A truth twice the prior gives $t_w = \ln 2 \approx 0.693$; half gives $t_w = \ln 0.5 \approx -0.693$. Expansion and shrinkage penalties symmetrize in log domain, bounding extreme-ratio gradient shocks.

### Pixel-to-four-edge distance representation

As anchor-free detection spread, box parameterization moved from preset-shape offsets to radial distances rooted directly at feature-point geometry. Part 1 covers multi-scale grid-point prediction mechanics; see [prediction carriers](./prediction-carrier).

For a sampling point projecting to image coordinates $(p_x, p_y)$ that falls inside some true box $[x_1, y_1, x_2, y_2]$, the head assigns the Euclidean distance vector $\boldsymbol{d} = (l, t, r, b)$ to the four physical borders:

$$
\begin{aligned}
l &= p_x - x_1, \quad &t = p_y - y_1 \\
r &= x_2 - p_x, \quad &b = y_2 - p_y
\end{aligned}
$$

For that distant $16\times 16$ street sign with image borders $[x_1=500, y_1=100, x_2=516, y_2=116]$, a prediction point mapping to $(508, 108)$ — dead center — owns the true distance vector $(l=8, t=8, r=8, b=8)$.

The representation drops all prior-box hyperparameters. Heads emit four nonnegative scalars; simple addition and subtraction recover absolute predicted borders:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat{x}_1 &= p_x - \hat{l}, \quad &\hat{y}_1 = p_y - \hat{t} \\
\hat{x}_2 &= p_x + \hat{r}, \quad &\hat{y}_2 = p_y + \hat{b}
\end{aligned}
$$

Parameterization unifies numeric scales and establishes geometric mapping. It only solves value encoding and decoding. How deviation between predictions and labels should be measured is localization optimization's core contradiction.

## The independent-coordinate-loss defect: $L_1$ decoupled from overlap

Early deep-learning practice borrowed standard regression handling for box localization, applying $L_1$, Smooth $L_1$, or mean-squared ($L_2$) losses independently per coordinate component.

Smooth $L_1$ and the total four-coordinate localization loss:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{Smooth}_{L_1}(x) &= \begin{cases}
0.5 x^2 & |x| < 1 \\
|x| - 0.5 & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases} \\
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{coord}} &= \sum_{i \in \{l, t, r, b\}} \mathrm{Smooth}_{L_1}\bigl(\hat{d}_i - d_i\bigr)
\end{aligned}
$$

Splitting boxes into four independently penalized variables is geometrically flawed by birth.

### Scale-blind geometric sensitivity

Box quality never depends on single-coordinate absolute deviation but on deviation relative to the object's own size.

Consider two scenes:

1. The distant $16\times 16$ sign with truth $(8, 8, 8, 8)$. If the network predicts left distance $\hat{l} = 4$ with the other three sides perfect, single-edge error is 4 pixels and total absolute coordinate error is 4 pixels. But horizontal span shrinks from 16 to 12 pixels, and IoU crashes from 1.0 straight to $0.75$.
2. A nearby $200\times 100$-pixel pedestrian with truth $(50, 100, 50, 100)$. The same 4-pixel left-distance miss ($\hat{l} = 46$) produces the identical 4-pixel total error and the identical Smooth $L_1$ penalty as the sign. Yet against 100-pixel width, 4 pixels indent only $4\%$, leaving IoU above $0.96$.

Independent coordinate losses cannot see size. Identical absolute pixel misses devastate small objects morphologically and merely jitter large ones. Relative parameterization mitigates this only through static prior-to-object scale matching — never by directly linking loss to overlap.

### Isolated four-component optimization breaks geometric coupling

A rectangle's shape is jointly decided by four coupled components; naive summation ignores correlations between edge errors.

For a $20\times 20$ true box:

- Case A: center shift pushes both side edges 2 pixels the same direction ($\Delta l = +2, \Delta r = -2$), total $L_1$ error 4 pixels. Rigid translation keeps overlap large and IoU high.
- Case B: all four edges shrink inward 1 pixel each ($\Delta l = -1, \Delta t = -1, \Delta r = -1, \Delta b = -1$), total $L_1$ error also 4 pixels. But length and width shrink together, area falling 400 to 324, IoU collapsing.

Independent coordinate losses penalize both identically — equal summed absolute component gaps. Yet detection quality (mAP) stands entirely on IoU thresholds. Training strains to press four algebraic residuals while testing inspects 2D rectangle overlap areas. The disconnect pushed researchers toward geometry losses built directly on IoU.

## The IoU loss family: whole overlap to geometric penalties

Killing scale sensitivity and component mismatch at the root means optimizing the predicted box as one indivisible geometric whole.

With true region $B^{gt}$ and predicted region $B$, intersection area $|B \cap B^{gt}|$ and union area $|B \cup B^{gt}|$, IoU is their ratio:

$$
\mathrm{IoU} = \frac{|B \cap B^{gt}|}{|B \cup B^{gt}|}
$$

IoU owns excellent mathematics: strictly bounded in $[0, 1]$ with scale invariance. A 16-pixel sign and a 200-pixel pedestrian share identical IoU whenever their spatial coincidence ratios match.

The most direct loss is $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IoU}} = 1 - \mathrm{IoU}$: zero at perfect coincidence, rising linearly as overlap falls.

But basic IoU loss carries a fatal mathematical defect: **vanishing gradients at zero intersection**.

:::caution[IoU loss gradient death]
When predicted box $B$ and truth $B^{gt}$ are fully disjoint, $|B \cap B^{gt}| = 0$, so $\mathrm{IoU} = 0$ and loss pins at $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IoU}} = 1$. Every prediction-parameter partial derivative is zero; backpropagation offers no usable gradient. Early in training, or when deep grids predict distant objects, predictions easily detach from truths entirely — and optimization stalls outright.
:::

Later work injected geometric penalty terms into IoU to restore vanishing gradients.

### GIoU: minimum closure and gap penalty

To fix zero derivatives on disjoint boxes, Generalized IoU (GIoU) introduces the **minimum enclosing rectangle** (the smallest convex closure $C$ containing both $B$ and $B^{gt}$):

$$
\mathrm{GIoU} = \mathrm{IoU} - \frac{|C \setminus (B \cup B^{gt})|}{|C|}
$$

with loss $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GIoU}} = 1 - \mathrm{GIoU}$. The extra term measures the **gap-area ratio** inside the joint closure $C$ belonging to neither box.

For fully disjoint boxes, $\mathrm{IoU} = 0$ and the formula degrades to $\mathrm{GIoU} = -\frac{|C| - |B| - |B^{gt}|}{|C|}$.

GIoU then sits negative in $(-1, 0]$. Far-apart predictions inflate closure $C$ enormously, gap ratio approaches 1, $\mathrm{GIoU} \to -1$, loss near 2; as predictions approach truth, $C$ shrinks fast, gap ratio falls, loss follows. Since closure area $|C|$ derives directly from extreme coordinates of both boxes' outer borders, gradients flow smoothly to prediction heads in disjoint states, actively pushing predictions toward truths until overlap forms.

GIoU still degenerates: when one box fully contains the other, their minimum closure $C$ equals the outer box itself. Then $|C| - |B \cup B^{gt}| = 0$, the extra penalty pins at zero, and the formula collapses to plain IoU. Inside such containment, inner-box translations that stay in-bounds escape GIoU's center-drift perception.

### Center-distance and shape penalties: DIoU and CIoU

For containment degeneration, DIoU (Distance-IoU) bypasses closure gaps, pulling the two boxes' center Euclidean distance straight into the loss:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DIoU}} = 1 - \mathrm{IoU} + \frac{\rho^2(b, b^{gt})}{c^2}
$$

where $b$ and $b^{gt}$ are predicted and true geometric centers, $\rho(\cdot)$ is Euclidean distance, and $c$ is the minimum-closure diagonal covering both boxes. DIoU's logic is blunt: overlap or containment regardless, the center-distance term gravitates continuously, dragging predicted centers horizontally and vertically onto target centers.

Later CIoU adds an aspect-ratio penalty atop DIoU: $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CIoU}} = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DIoU}} + \alpha v$ with $v = \frac{4}{\pi^2}\bigl(\arctan\frac{w^{gt}}{h^{gt}} - \arctan\frac{w}{h}\bigr)^2$ and balancing weight $\alpha$.

One explicit verdict: CIoU constrains overlap area, center distance, and aspect ratio together, but its aspect-ratio term is not solid. $v$ measures relative deviation of the width-height ratio ($\frac{w}{h}$), never independently constraining absolute width and height scales — proportionally co-scaled boxes keep the ratio and defeat the penalty. Worse, inverse-trigonometric functions complicate the derivative chain; in practice the aspect-ratio term's effective gradient contribution stays marginal and never became localization optimization's main line.

From Smooth $L_1$ through GIoU and DIoU, this stage firmly established IoU-geometry-led box regression. Yet all these geometric losses share one substrate premise: every box border is a spatially certain single point (Dirac delta distribution), with the network emitting exact continuous values.

Real visual data routinely breaks the determinism assumption.

## Border uncertainty: point estimates to distribution modeling

Look again at those two side-by-side interlaced pedestrians at the street scene's center.

When one pedestrian's arm occludes the other's torso, even trained annotators disagree by pixels on where the occluded pedestrian's right border belongs — inside the occluding arm or extrapolated to the possible body edge. And around that distant $16\times 16$ sign, lens depth-of-field blur and edge chromatic aberration weave sign edges into background foliage as a smooth translucent pixel band.

In such scenes, true object borders are not mathematically infinitely sharp lines. Fuzziness and semantic ambiguity are native.

Forcing deterministic network outputs makes the network guess hard inside fuzzy zones, oscillating between contradictory edge features. DFL (Distribution Focal Loss) overthrows point estimation, recasting border regression as probability-distribution prediction.

### DFL: discrete-interval distribution representation

DFL stops predicting a continuous distance scalar $y$ directly. Instead it discretizes the continuous regression range into fixed-interval discrete values (bins).

With maximum regression distance $y_{\max}$, the system evenly divides it into integer points $\mathcal{S} = \{0, 1, 2, \dots, n\}$. For any edge's distance prediction, the network emits not one scalar but unnormalized log-probability logits over discrete set $\mathcal{S}$. Softmax converts them into the edge's positional probability distribution $\boldsymbol{P} = (p_0, p_1, \dots, p_n)$ with $\sum_{i=0}^n p_i = 1$.

The restored continuous distance estimate $\hat{y}$ is the distribution's **expectation** over discrete support:

$$
\hat{y} = \sum_{i=0}^n i \cdot p_i
$$

The design is elegant: discrete probability distributions model border uncertainty inside the network, while outward it still emits one continuous differentiable expectation scalar $\hat{y}$ — pluggable into IoU, GIoU, and other geometric losses for end-to-end training without friction.

```text title="Point estimation versus distribution regression"
Continuous regression (point estimate):
True y=7.3 ────> [ network emits single scalar: 7.3 ] ────> border fuzziness inexpressible

Distribution regression (DFL discrete distribution):
True y=7.3 ────> discrete bins: [0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, ... , n]
                                           │  │
                                target weights: 0.7 0.3
                                           ▼  ▼
                               network emits: [p0, p1, ... p7, p8, ... pn]
                                           │
                                           ▼ expectation
                               restored: y_hat = Σ (i * p_i)
```

For supervision signals, when an edge's continuous true distance $y$ falls between adjacent integer indices $y_i$ and $y_{i+1}$ ($y_i \le y < y_{i+1}$, $y_{i+1} = y_i + 1$), DFL skips crude integer rounding and linearly splits truth mass onto the two nearest bins:

- discrete position $y_i$ receives target probability $y_{i+1} - y$;
- discrete position $y_{i+1}$ receives $y - y_i$.

For that distant sign whose edge true distance on the current grid is $y = 7.3$ — between bins 7 and 8 — bin 7 gets target weight $8 - 7.3 = 0.7$ and bin 8 gets $7.3 - 7 = 0.3$.

DFL loss runs cross-entropy against exactly those two neighboring bins:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DFL}}(p_i, p_{i+1}) = -\bigl((y_{i+1} - y)\ln(p_i) + (y - y_i)\ln(p_{i+1})\bigr)
$$

The $y = 7.3$ example above is $-\bigl(0.7 \ln(p_7) + 0.3 \ln(p_8)\bigr)$.

:::tip[Two consequences of DFL supervision]
First, distribution shape reflects border confidence: razor-sharp borders (high-contrast sign-sky junctions) concentrate predicted mass into a sharp peak near truth (e.g. $p_7$ and $p_8$ holding nearly all probability, other bins near zero); heavily occluded or fuzzy-transition borders (interlaced pedestrians' arm edges) spontaneously flatten or bimodalize — distribution variance directly expresses the network's geometric position uncertainty. Second, unreliable far discrete values are avoided: ordinary classification losses scatter distributions across full space, while DFL constrains cross-entropy to the two bins nearest truth, forcing all probability mass into truth's tiny local neighborhood so far-off extreme bins cannot drag the expectation during backpropagation.
:::

### FDR: refined distribution regression

Under DFL, bin partitions are fixed and uniform (usually step 1, range 0–16). Uniform partitioning serves common objects well but hits a compute-versus-precision bottleneck at extremes: denser bins for sub-pixel precision multiply head channels; coarse bins cap expectation interpolation precision.

D-FINE answers with Fine-grained Distribution Refinement (FDR).

FDR's core judgment: **border-distribution resolution should never use coarse uniform grids one-shot across all levels, but refine progressively along network depth.**

At shallow or initial stages, the network predicts a coarse discrete distribution over the border's rough range, locking the base integer interval; deeper into finer prediction stages, later layers stop predicting the whole large-range global distribution from scratch and instead predict a higher-resolution, narrower local residual-refinement distribution around the previously predicted center.

Decomposing global large-range discrete distributions into "coarse localization distribution + layer-wise residual refinement distributions", FDR pushes border probability representation to higher spatial resolution without greatly growing total bin counts. Layer-progressive refined distribution modeling represents the current deepening direction of border-uncertainty modeling.

## After box supervision: the classification–localization split

Box representation and loss supervision now form a complete loop:

From gradient-imbalancing absolute pixels to scale-erasing relative parameterization; from geometry-splitting Smooth $L_1$ detached from evaluation metrics to whole-binding, scale-invariant IoU-series geometric losses; to DFL and layer-refined distributions (FDR) probabilistically modeling border fuzziness past deterministic point-estimate assumptions. Localization losses finally let networks hug predicted boxes onto object edges in training the way evaluation actually measures.

Yet on any single feature map, a complete detection prediction carries class predictions alongside positional geometry.

Even with every grid's border regression optimized to the extreme, one fatal structural fracture remains: **the branch predicting box geometric quality and the branch predicting object class stay largely mutually isolated.**

In street imagery, a shallow grid far from a pedestrian's center may see only a distinctive clothing texture patch, prompting a rash $0.95$ classification confidence from the classification head; but sitting on the object edge without global contour view, its regressed box quality is awful — IoU $0.3$ with the true pedestrian. Conversely, another grid in the pedestrian's core region predicts an exquisite IoU-$0.90$ box yet scores only $0.60$ classification confidence under local shadow interference.

Entering post-processing, traditional systems trust the $0.95$ score first, directly suppressing and eliminating the low-confidence but high-precision quality prediction.

Localization losses solved "how to compute boxes accurately" but cannot solve "how does the network know how accurate its boxes are". The deep misalignment between classification scores and true box geometric quality became the key barrier to further detection gains. Dismantling and resolving it belongs to [Part 5: Classification Confidence and Detection Heads](./classification-head).
